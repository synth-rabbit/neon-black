# The Second Copy — Aftermath

The denouement of [[acquisition]] (Core p. 292): the reflection, the fallout, and the one decision this job exists to put in front of the table. Load Off before downtime (Core p. 124).

## Rewards (Core p. 293)

**Narrative first.** A crew that started this Series as five names on a list nobody was paying to collect now knows something about themselves that no corporation intended anyone to read, and holds — or held — the object that proves it. That is the reward, and it is the reason the job was worth doing whatever else went wrong.

**Cash.** The fee from [[margit-nakagawa|Margit Nakagawa]]'s firm, less [[marisol-okonkwo|Tally]]'s percentage and whatever the [[weighhouse-line-of-credit]] took back. Give the crew a solvency status against the depth of the employer's pockets, which are a law firm's and not a corporation's: ***flush-2*** on delivery; ***lots-to-spend-3*** if they also sold the two pages or took the Carters' price; ***scraping-by-2*** and the Slate un-cleared if they lost the volume and delivered nothing. Selling the volume to Barrow pays best in the moment and is the only outcome that leaves them with no proof of anything.

[[secret-war-goes-public]] moves **only** on a public, witnessed event (*The Noise Absorbs*). A quiet theft in a warehouse in a district where nobody official comes down adds nothing, and should not.

With Job 0 at 0 and Job 1 at 0–4, a table that has played every job loud can reach `public-war-6` at the end of this job — the countdown working as written, less whatever *Cleanup Crews* removed between jobs. ## Denouement questions (Core p. 292)

Asked of the players, not answered by this file.

1. Which of you opened the volume, and which of you said leave it?
2. You read a line that had your work line's number in it. What did you do with your face?
3. Somebody bought the night you got out. Does that make it worth less?
4. What did you do about [[ileana-boakye|Ileana Boakye]] — and would you do it again for the same money?
5. Barrow offered a fair price and meant it. What did it cost to say no, and who said it?
6. You have been carrying *not worth a bounty* since the first session. Is being worth one unit better or worse?
7. Who else has read those pages now, and did any of you choose that?
8. If [[halima-boyce]] gave you one thing, which did you take — the name, or the paper? Who wanted the other one?

## The pivot statement

The book's own model (Core p. 281): *a dramatic twist in an early job could easily be the launching point for a series based around a Key Player who was ultimately behind the job, turning a Paycheck series into a Misfits one as everyone around the table comes to agree that this Key Player needs to be taken down.* **Everyone around the table.** Not this file, and not the MC.

So: three doors are open and one of them is the door the crew are already standing in. Nothing in the vault pushes them through any of them, and the Series works from any of the four.

### Stay Paycheck

**What it looks like.** Jobs come through Tally, the crew get paid, and the two pages go in a box under a bed. The Series stays episodic and the arcs stay personal, which is what a Paycheck Series is for (Core p. 281): the crew are not attached to any of these institutions, and each of them is still changed by what they read.
**What it costs.** Somebody else is going to do something with those pages eventually, and the crew will hear about it. The standing-account number stays a number, and *worth one unit* stays a thing they know about themselves and do not act on.
**What the vault gives it.** Everything. The Key Players' hooks, the reuse map, the Weighhouse, and a city that does not care.

### Misfits against [[upstart|Continuity Risk & Response]]

**The reading that gets them here.** *Somebody bought the night we became people, eleven days in advance, off a published fee schedule, and wrote us into the schedule as released site population.* If the GM has adopted the package's proposal, the standing account is Continuity's; if not, the account is whoever the GM says, and this branch belongs to them instead.
**What it costs.** Continuity is the strongest thing in the city that can move without permission, it is exactly as legal as the police, it holds the ward every Domain in Palisade sits in, and it currently knows the crew as, at most, a line in a cleanup report. Choosing it means being noticed by it, which is a decision that cannot be taken back, and the first thing that goes is the Weighhouse's neutrality.
**What the Series becomes.** A campaign about proving a pattern rather than winning a fight: advance concurrences, monitored Domains, the pages that are only in the scan set, and a firm whose method is to be first at emergencies it arranged. **[OPEN]** (OQ-17) — what Continuity *is* stays untouched, and the Series does not need it to be answered to run.

### Misfits against [[corp-c|AP&I]]

**The reading that gets them here.** *We were never released. Our accounts were sold on for a unit each, and a Standing Account does not close.* The escapee list said no bounty; the transfer schedule says they were worth exactly one unit to a purchaser, which is a different and worse sentence.
**What it costs.** AP&I is the Big Three member with the most to lose from a war and the deepest reach into ordinary life — a warranty tag under a forearm, a fitting theatre, a clinic on [[suture-row]]. Choosing it means every piece of AP&I chrome any PC is carrying becomes a liability, and it means the [[escapee-recovery-desk]] eventually stops being patient.
**What the Series becomes.** A campaign about a lending book: the Ledger, the fifth block, the hundred and four accounts, and the question of what happens when somebody makes a written-off crew expensive on purpose. **[OPEN]** (OQ-41) becomes the crew's lever — AP&I still cannot say out loud what happened at the camp.

### Misfits against [[government|the Chancery]]

**The reading that gets them here.** *None of it was illegal. The window was on a fee schedule, the transfer was a filing, and the only reason any of it worked is that the record can be sold twice.* The Chancery did not sell them; it sold the possibility.
**What it costs.** The Chancery is the cheapest Key Player to hurt and the only one whose collapse helps every other Key Player at once. It is also the crew's only door to paper — no more looks at lists, no more struck names, no more concurrences — and [[halima-boyce]] is inside the thing they would be taking down.
**What the Series becomes.** A campaign about the record itself: the counterparts, the audit nobody can survive, and a government that profits from a war it will not name ([[government]]). The crew would be trying to make one true page exist in Palisade, which is either the smallest ambition in the Series or the largest.

### How the table gets there

The job hands the table three sentences and no verdict: *they bought the night*, *they sold us*, *it was all on a price list*. [[acquisition-09-what-a-page-is-worth]] and the denouement questions above are where the players say one of them out loud, or do not. Whichever sentence the table keeps returning to names the Key Player that becomes central, which is the shape the book describes (Core p. 281): everyone around the table coming to agree. If none of them lands, the Series is Paycheck and the pages are a thing the crew know — a legitimate outcome, and not a failed pivot.
